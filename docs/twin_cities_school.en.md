# The Twin Cities School — The lab before D&D

## 1. Purpose of the research

The popular history of *Dungeons & Dragons* tends to condense around two names:

Dave Arneson and Gary Gygax.

The attribution is understandable: both are credited as co-creators of the game published in 1974.

However, going back a few years reveals a much more collective story.

Before *Blackmoor*, before *Dungeons & Dragons*, and before TSR, there was an active community of wargamers in the Minneapolis–St. Paul, Minnesota area.

Within it participated people like:

- David Wesely;
- Dave Arneson;
- David Megarry;
- Greg Svenson;
- John Snider;
- Richard Snider;
- Duane Jenkins;
- and other players and designers.

They did not form a "school" in the academic sense nor necessarily used that term to describe themselves.

In RPG Archaeology, we will provisionally use:

**The Twin Cities School**

as a label to study that ecosystem of players, referees, and designers who, through successive wargaming experiments, developed practices that would prove fundamental for the birth of the RPG.

The central question will be:

> What happened in the Twin Cities for a group of wargamers to start playing in a way that no longer fit entirely within traditional wargaming?

## 2. A caution: do not look for a single inventor

A genealogy overly simplified could be written as:

```text
WARGAMES
    ↓
ARNEson
    ↓
BLACKMOOR
    ↓
D&D
```

But this representation eliminates precisely the experimental environment that made *Blackmoor* possible.

Available historical evidence shows exchange of ideas between players, constant rule modification, and prior experiments that introduced elements that would later be recognizable in the RPG.

Therefore, this research proceeds from a different methodological hypothesis:

**The RPG did not appear through a single punctual invention. It arose through accumulation, recombination, and stabilization of different gameplay practices.**

This does not eliminate the individual importance of Arneson, Wesley, Gygax, or others.

It allows understanding what each one actually did.

## 3. The Twin Cities and wargaming

During the 1960s, an active community of war game enthusiasts existed in Minneapolis–St. Paul.

Like in other American communities of the period, its participants:

- played commercial wargames;
- designed variants;
- wrote their own rules;
- organized campaigns;
- exchanged ideas;
- participated in clubs;
- used correspondence and amateur publications.

The wargaming of that time should not be imagined only as consumers playing finished products.

An important part of the hobby was precisely modifying the game.

```text
RULESET
    ↓
GAME SESSION
    ↓
PROBLEM / IDEA
    ↓
HOUSE RULE
    ↓
VARIANT
    ↓
NEW GAME SESSION
```

This culture of modification is fundamental to understanding what happens next.

## 4. David Wesely

One of the central figures in this environment was David Wesely.

Wesley was a wargamer and amateur designer, but he was also deeply interested in a problem that had accompanied wargaming since *Kriegsspiel*:

> How can a referee resolve situations that the rules have not explicitly anticipated?

That problem led him toward adjudication systems and later to the development of *Strategos*.

## 5. Strategos and the referee tradition

The name *Strategos* has a prior genealogy.

In 1880, the American officer Charles A. L. Totten had published:

*Strategos: A Series of American Games of War.*

The work belonged to the American adaptation tradition of the European *Kriegsspiel*.

Decades later, Wesley recovered ideas related to this tradition of adjudicated games for his own experiments.

The important element for our genealogy is not a direct mechanical transmission of all its rules.

It is the survival of a figure:

the referee.

In traditional wargaming, the referee could:

- maintain hidden information;
- know the complete state of the situation;
- receive orders from players;
- interpret rules;
- resolve ambiguous situations;
- communicate only the information available to each participant.

We can represent this model:

```text
PLAYER A ──┐
             │
             ▼
          REFEREE
             ▲
             │
PLAYER B ──┘
             │
             ▼
          REAL STATE
          OF THE SCENARIO
```
└── improvise
```

This mechanism will be crucial for what happens next.

## 6. Braunstein

In 1969, David Wesley organized an experimental game that would later be known as *Braunstein*.

The scenario represented a fictional German city during a Napoleonic military context.

The experiment began as a variation on traditional war games.

But Wesley introduced a decisive modification.

Instead of limiting all participants to controlling military forces, different participants received individual roles within the city.

They could represent, for example:

- officers;
- local authorities;
- bankers;
- students;
- revolutionaries;
- characters with their own interests.

Each participant could possess:

- different information;
- personal objectives;
- different relationships with others;
- possibilities of action not fully anticipated by the rules.

The center of the game began to shift.

It was no longer only:

> What orders do you give to your forces?

Now it could be:

> What does your character do?

## 7. The productive accident

One of the most important features of *Braunstein* is that much of what would later be interesting was not necessarily planned as the birth of a new genre.

Participants began to:

- negotiate;
- deceive each other;
- conspire;
- separate;
- pursue personal objectives;
- perform actions that Wesley had not anticipated.

This produced a situation apparently problematic.

The available rules could not describe all possible actions.

But Wesley had a tool from adjudicated wargaming:

the referee.

When a player declared an unanticipated action, Wesley could decide what happened.

```text
PLAYER
   │
   │ "I want to do X"
   ▼
REFEREE
   │
   │ interprets situation
   │ applies rules if they exist
   │ adjudicates if they don't
   ▼
WORLD RESPONDS
```

Here appears one of the fundamental principles of the tabletop RPG:

**The space of possible actions can be greater than the space of written rules.**

The referee covers the difference.

## 8. From unit control to individual role

The change seems small:

```text
BEFORE


Player
   ↓
Army / unit

versus:

BRAUNSTEIN


``text
Player
   ↓
Individual
```

But its consequences are enormous.

When the player controls an individual, issues that may be secondary in a traditional wargame begin to matter:

- identity;
- motivations;
- personal information;
- relationships;
- concrete positioning;
- survival;
- individual decisions.

The participant stops observing the battle exclusively from above.

They begin to inhabit the situation from within.

We don't yet necessarily have a complete RPG.

But one of its fundamental perspective shifts is already present.

## 9. Braunstein was not yet Dungeons & Dragons

We should avoid interpreting *Braunstein* retrospectively as if Wesley had organized a modern RPG session.

**DOCUMENTED FACT**

>*Braunstein* introduced individual roles, differentiated objectives, incomplete information, and referee adjudication.

**HISTORICAL INFERENCE**

>These characteristics constitute clear antecedents of practices later central to role-playing games.

**CAUTION**

> This does not mean *Braunstein* already contained all the elements that would later define the RPG:

- persistent campaign;
- persistent character developed through numerous sessions;
- systematic progression;
- dungeon crawl;
- classes;
- levels;
- experience system;
- modern adventurer party structure.

It is precisely the subsequent evolution that interests us.

## 10. The experiment continues

The success —or productive chaos— of *Braunstein* led to new sessions and variants.

Other participants began experimenting with the format.

This is important because it changes our genealogy.

We don't have:

```text
Wesley
   ↓
invents system
   ↓
Arneson copies it
```

We have something more like:

```text
WESELY
   │
   ▼
BRAUNSTEIN
   │
   ▼
PLAYERS
   │
   ├── prueban
   ├── modifican
   ├── reinterpretan
   └── crean variantes
        │
        ▼
    NUEVOS EXPERIMENTOS
```

Innovation began to circulate within the group.

## 11. Dave Arneson steps onto the scene

Dave Arneson belonged to this community and participated in Wesley's experiments.

Later he began to develop his own games.

In April 1971 there is contemporary documentation that Arneson was preparing what he described as a:

> **"medieval Braunstein"**

The expression is extraordinarily important.

It provides us with a documentary link between:

```text
BRAUNSTEIN
     ↓
MEDIEVAL BRAUNSTEIN
     ↓
BLACKMOOR
```

We don't need to reconstruct retrospectively the entire relationship.

The participants themselves were using *Braunstein*'s vocabulary to describe the new experiment.

## 12. Blackmoor

Arneson's experiment ended up becoming *Blackmoor*.

Here, several ideas that had appeared earlier began to combine within a more stable structure.

*Blackmoor* progressively incorporated:

- individual characters;
- continuity between sessions;
- persistent world;
- exploration;
- combat;
- treasures;
- progression;
- adventures;
- factions;
- referee;
- rules modified and added during play.

The fundamental change relative to *Braunstein* could be summarized as:

```text
BRAUNSTEIN


situation
   ↓
individual character
   ↓
objective
   ↓
resolution

versus:

BLACKMOOR


individual character
      ↓
adventure
      ↓
survives
      ↓
preserves state
      ↓
progresses
      ↓
returns
      ↓
new adventure
      ↓
same world
```

Persistence appeared.

And with it, the nature of the game changed deeply.

## 13. Blackmoor as crystallization

Here we must be especially precise.

Saying:

> "Dave Arneson invented the RPG."

is an overly strong statement for the state of our research.

But saying:

> "Blackmoor constitutes one of the first campaigns where numerous fundamental elements of the RPG appear combined in a persistent and recognizable way."

is much more defensible.

Later testimonies from Twin Cities participants attribute to Arneson a central role in that crystallization.

An important reason is that Arneson carried the experiment toward a continued campaign and left documentation about it.

That's why *Blackmoor* occupies a different position from the previous *Braunstein*.

Not necessarily because each element was invented there.

But because there many of them began to work together.

## 14. David Megarry

Another fundamental participant was David Megarry.

Megarry played in *Blackmoor* as H. W. Dumbo and participated in dungeon expeditions.

His experience produced later another historically important artifact:

*Dungeon!*

The board game reduced part of the dungeon crawl experience to a much more formalized structure:

```text
DUNGEON
   ↓
habitaciones
   ↓
monstruos
   ↓
combate
   ↓
tesoro
   ↓
progresión por el espacio
```

This is fascinating for RPG Archaeology.

*Blackmoor* was generating different descendants.

One would end up contributing to *D&D*.

Another could abstract the dungeon crawl toward a board game.

The same experience was being interpreted from different perspectives.

## 15. Megarry's maps

Megarry also preserved maps made during his *Blackmoor* explorations.

These documents constitute one of our most interesting sources.

They don't simply represent a dungeon designed afterward.

They represent the spatial perception of a player during an early campaign before *D&D* was published.

Megarry later explained to Boggs that players:

- didn't see Arneson's map;
- received descriptions;
- heard distances;
- received room dimensions;
- knew exits and stairs through description;
- drew their own sketches on graph paper.

Therefore:

```text
ARNEson'S MAP
      ↓
DESCRIPTION
      ↓
PERCEPTION
      ↓
SKETCH
      ↓
MEGARRY'S MAP
```

## 16. Greg Svenson and Tonisborg

Greg Svenson, another participant in the Twin Cities environment and early *Blackmoor* player, developed his own dungeon:

*Tonisborg*.

Its importance for our genealogy is considerable.

*Tonisborg* demonstrates that the dungeon was not limited to:

> "the thing that Dave Arneson runs."

Other participants began to build and run their own adventure spaces.

This implies a transfer of knowledge.

```text
ARNEson
   ↓
BLACKMOOR
   ↓
PLAYERS
   ↓
comprenden el formato
   ↓
CREAN SUS PROPIOS DUNGEONS
```

When a practice can be reproduced by others, we are dealing with something much more important than a personal peculiarity of the original referee.

We are witnessing the emergence of a transmittable form of play.

## 17. The referee as technology

One of the most interesting discoveries of our entire genealogy is the continuity of the referee figure.

We can trace approximately this line:

```text
KRIEGSSPIEL
     │
     ▼
UMPIRE
interprets military situation
     │
     ▼
FREE KRIEGSSPIEL
judgment > exhaustive rule
     │
     ▼
WESELY
BRAUNSTEIN
     │
     ▼
REFEREE
resolves individual actions
     │
     ▼
ARNEson
BLACKMOOR
     │
     ▼
REFEREE
mantiene mundo persistente
```

This doesn't mean there is a perfect linear transmission between each element.

But a conceptual continuity does appear:

a human situated between the rules and the world can interpret actions that the system hasn't fully formalized.

This capability will be fundamental for the RPG.

## 18. Freedom appears before the rules

In a completely closed game:

```text
POSSIBLE ACTIONS
      =
RULEED ACTIONS
```

In the Twin Cities experiments, something different begins to appear:

```text
POSSIBLE ACTIONS
      >
RULEED ACTIONS
```

The player can try something that no one wrote beforehand.

The referee decides how to respond.

**This simple inequality may be one of the most important conceptual transformations of the entire RPG genealogy.**

It allows the player to stop selecting only options available within the system.

They can begin to declare intentions.

## 19. Designing through play

Another common pattern seems to be the absence of a rigid separation between:

```text
DESIGN
  ↓
FINISHED PRODUCT
  ↓
PLAYTEST
```

Practice seems much more iterative:

```text
IDEA
  ↓
GAME SESSION
  ↓
UNEXPECTED SITUATION
  ↓
ADJUDICATION
  ↓
NEW RULE
  ↓
NEXT SESSION
```

In other words:

**the game was being discovered while being played.**

This characteristic will be especially visible in *Blackmoor*.

## 20. An oral culture

There is also an important historiographic problem.

Much of these innovations did not initially appear in commercial manuals.

They circulated through:

- game sessions;
- conversations;
- correspondence;
- clubs;
- fanzines;
- private notes;
- typed rules;
- demonstrations.

This explains why reconstructing the genealogy is so difficult.

An idea could exist for months before being documented.

Another could be used by several participants without us knowing exactly who introduced it first.

And decades later, protagonists' memories might differ.

Therefore, we must always distinguish:

first known documented evidence

from

first actual use.

They are not necessarily the same.

## 21. Secrets of Blackmoor

The *Secrets of Blackmoor* documentary is a particularly interesting source for reconstructing this environment.

[Secrets of Blackmoor — sitio oficial](https://secretsofblackmoor.com/)

Its main value for RPG Archaeology lies in gathering later testimonies from numerous participants linked to the original *Blackmoor* scene and the Twin Cities.

Among them appear memories about:

- *Braunstein*;
- Wesley;
- Arneson;
- *Blackmoor*;
- early campaigns;
- referee methods;
- rule evolution;
- Twin Cities player culture.

### SOURCE CLASSIFICATION

It should be treated mainly as:

**POSTERIOR TESTIMONY OF PARTICIPANTS**

and not as contemporary documentation of 1969–1971.

This doesn't reduce its enormous value.

It simply requires that specific claims be contrasted with:

- contemporary documents;
- fanzines;
- maps;
- manuscripts;
- correspondence;
- early publications.

## 22. A collective innovation

After studying this ecosystem, it becomes difficult to maintain a purely individual genealogy.

A more adequate representation would be:

```text

       WESLEY                OTHERS

*Blackmoor* occupies the center because it ended up being the historically decisive branch for *D&D*.

But its roots belong to an ecosystem.

## 23. And meanwhile, in Lake Geneva...

The story is not yet complete.

While the Twin Cities experimented with characters, referees, and campaigns, another wargaming community was developing different systems.

In Wisconsin, Jeff Perren and Gary Gygax had worked on medieval rules that would be published in 1971 as:

*Chainmail*.

Its evolution followed another path:

```text
WARGAMING
    ↓
MEDIEVAL COMBAT
    ↓
FORMALIZED RULES
    ↓
FANTASY SUPPLEMENT
    ↓
HEROES / WIZARDS / MONSTERS
```

So we have two contemporary lines:

```text
TWIN CITIES                     LAKE GENEVA

Wesley                           Perren
   │                               │
Braunstein                        │
   │                               ▼
Arneson                       Perren + Gygax
   │                               │
Blackmoor                      Chainmail
   │                               │
   │                               │
   └───────────────┐ ┌─────────────┘
                   ▼ ▼
                    ?
```

That question mark will eventually be called:

*Dungeons & Dragons*.

## 24. They were not two isolated worlds

Nor should we interpret Twin Cities and Lake Geneva as two completely independent laboratories.

American wargaming communities were connected through:

- associations;
- conventions;
- correspondence;
- fanzines;
- publications;
- shared campaigns.

Dave Arneson belonged to the *Castle & Crusade Society*, organization founded by Gary Gygax and Robert Kuntz.

There was, therefore, circulation between both environments before *D&D* existed.

The real genealogy resembles more a network than a tree.

## 25. From experimental community to product

Here appears a transition that must be studied in the document dedicated to *D&D*.

The Twin Cities culture worked extraordinarily well to generate new forms of play.

But a home campaign could depend enormously on implicit knowledge.

*Blackmoor* could work because:

**Dave Arneson was there.**

A commercial product needed to solve another problem:

> How can someone who never met Arneson reproduce a similar experience?

That required turning part of the oral knowledge into:

- rules;
- procedures;
- tables;
- examples;
- terminology;
- documentation.

Here will begin to matter especially the convergence with Gary Gygax and his experience writing and publishing rules.

## 26. The authorship question

This genealogy also requires careful use of the word "inventor."

We can identify important contributions:

**David Wesley**

>*Braunstein* and the use of individual characters with their own objectives within an adjudicated scenario.

**Dave Arneson**

>The transformation and consolidation of those practices within a persistent fantasy campaign that would become *Blackmoor*.

**David Megarry**

>Participation in *Blackmoor*, early cartography, and later abstraction of the dungeon crawl in *Dungeon!*.

**Greg Svenson**

>Early participation and reproduction of the format through *Tonisborg*.

**Gary Gygax and Jeff Perren**

>Parallel development of *Chainmail*, providing another important vocabulary of medieval and fantasy rules.

But no list fully captures the process.

Innovation circulated.

## 27. Did Arneson invent the RPG?

After this research, we can respond with greater precision.

**TOO STRONG FORMULATION**

>Dave Arneson alone invented the role-playing game.

**TOO WEAK FORMULATION**

>Arneson simply added fantasy to wargames.

**PROVISIONAL FORMULATION**

>Dave Arneson was the central figure in the consolidation of *Blackmoor*, one of the first campaigns known where different innovations from wargaming and *Braunstein* experiments combined in a persistent and recognizable form that was a direct antecedent of the modern RPG.

His importance does not require erasing Wesley or the others.

On the contrary.

Understanding the ecosystem allows appreciating better what concrete transformation Arneson actually performed.

## 28. Why Blackmoor and not Braunstein?

This is probably one of the fundamental questions.

*Braunstein* had introduced a decisive rupture:

the individual within the scenario.

*Blackmoor* seems to have added something equally important:

the individual continues to exist after the scenario.

That change introduces:

```text
CHARACTER
   +
PERSISTENCE
   +
WORLD
   +
PROGRESSION
   +
REFEREE
=
CAMPAIGN
```

When a session ends, the game doesn't necessarily reset to zero.

The character has history.

The world has memory.

And both can change.

There, we clearly begin to recognize the RPG.

## 29. Archaeological synthesis

The provisional conclusion of RPG Archaeology would be:

**The Twin Cities School was not a formal school, but an ecosystem of experimental wargamers in which different players began to expand the possibility space beyond traditional rules.**

David Wesley and *Braunstein* demonstrated that a participant could control an individual with their own objectives within an adjudicated scenario.

Dave Arneson turned part of that tradition into *Blackmoor*, where characters and world gained persistence.

*Blackmoor* players were not passive consumers.

They mapped, experimented, designed, and reproduced the new format.

David Megarry developed *Dungeon!*.

Greg Svenson developed *Tonisborg*.

Other participants contributed ideas, characters, rules, and experiences.

Therefore:

**Blackmoor did not appear in a vacuum. It was the most influential crystallization of a collective laboratory.**

## 31. Open questions

This first excavation leaves several issues for future research:

- What exact rules did the first *Braunstein* use?
- How did they evolve between Wesley's game sessions?
- What elements did Arneson adopt directly?
- Which ones did he modify?
- What innovations emerged specifically during *Blackmoor*?
- What contributions can be documented from other Twin Cities players?
- How far does *Tonisborg* reproduce *Blackmoor* procedures?
- What exact relationship exists between Megarry's experience in *Blackmoor* and *Dungeon!*?
- What contemporary documentation about the first sessions remains accessible?
- When did systematic character persistence begin exactly?
- When did mechanical progression appear?
- How were originally resolved movement, time, and exploration?
- What ideas traveled from Twin Cities to Lake Geneva before the *Blackmoor* demonstration?
- What elements that we later attribute to *D&D* were already circulating collectively among wargamers?

### Methodological note

"The Twin Cities School" is a designation used by RPG Archaeology to organize this research.

It does not pretend to assert that Wesley, Arneson, Megarry, Svenson, and the rest of the participants considered themselves members of a formal school.

The label describes an observable phenomenon:

a localized, connected, and experimental community in which different practices from wargaming evolved through play, adjudication, and exchange until producing some of the fundamental structures of the RPG.

And perhaps that is the main lesson of this excavation.

Before *Dungeons & Dragons* existed, there were people discovering how to play it without yet knowing that what they were doing needed a name.
