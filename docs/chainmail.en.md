# Chainmail — The rulebook before the role-playing game

## 1. Purpose of the research

Within the genealogy that leads to *Dungeons & Dragons*, *Chainmail* occupies a strange position.

It was not a role-playing game.

It had not been designed to explore dungeons through persistent characters nor to represent a world guided by a referee in the way that was being developed in *Blackmoor*.

It was fundamentally a medieval miniatures wargaming ruleset.

However, when *Dungeons & Dragons* appeared in 1974, its rules still explicitly referred to *Chainmail* as one of the possible systems for resolving combat.

Moreover, numerous concepts present in *Chainmail*, particularly in its *Fantasy Supplement*, would reappear transformed within *D&D*.

Therefore, it's tempting to build a simple genealogy:

```text
CHAINMAIL
    ↓
BLACKMOOR
    ↓
D&D
```

The history seems to have been much more complicated.

The aim of this research is to determine:

> What did *Chainmail* actually contribute to the process that ended up producing *Dungeons & Dragons*?

And especially:

> What led Gary Gygax to his collaboration with Dave Arneson before both began to turn their experiments into a publishable product?

## 2. Before Chainmail: Jeff Perren

The story of *Chainmail* does not begin only with Gary Gygax.

**DOCUMENTED FACT**

Jeff Perren, a member of the Lake Geneva wargamers community, had developed rules to represent medieval combats with miniatures.

Gygax took those rules as a starting point, expanded them considerably, and participated in their circulation within wargaming communities.

Early versions of these rules appeared before the commercial edition: a version titled *Geneva Medieval Miniatures* appeared in *Panzerfaust* in April 1970 and another version was published in *Domesday Book #5* in July of that year.

Therefore:

```text
JEFF PERREN
medieval rules
     │
     ▼
PERREN + GYGAX
expansion and development
     │
     ▼
circulation in the hobby
     │
     ▼
CHAINMAIL
```

This is important for RPG Archaeology.

If we want to avoid building a history based solely on great names, Perren must retain his place in the genealogy.

## 3. The Castle & Crusade Society

The development of these rules occurred within an extremely active enthusiasts community.

Gary Gygax and Robert Kuntz founded in 1970 the *Castle & Crusade Society*, an organization linked to the *International Federation of Wargamers* and dedicated especially to medieval wargaming.

Its publication, the *Domesday Book*, allowed circulating:

- rules;
- variants;
- campaigns;
- ideas;
- scenarios;
- correspondence between players.

The rules that would lead to *Chainmail* circulated precisely within this ecosystem before becoming a commercial product.

This reinforces an observation already appeared during our research on *Blackmoor*:

> The first RPGs did not emerge solely from individual designers. They arose from networks of enthusiasts who constantly modified, shared, and recombined systems.

The fanzine functioned almost as distributed development infrastructure.

## 4. Chainmail becomes a product

In 1970 Gary Gygax got in touch with Don Lowry, founder of *Guidon Games*.

Lowry was preparing a collection of rulesets called *Wargaming with Miniatures* and hired Gygax to work on it.

The first title published within that collection was precisely *Chainmail*.

**DOCUMENTED FACT**

The first commercial edition appeared in 1971, credited to:

Gary Gygax & Jeff Perren

and published by Guidon Games.

The edition had approximately sixty pages and contained something that would end up being much more historically important than its size suggested:

*Fantasy Supplement*

The first edition dedicated about fifteen pages to these fantasy rules.

## 5. What Chainmail really was

It is important not to retrospectively reinterpret *Chainmail* as a proto-RPG.

**DOCUMENTED FACT**

*Chainmail* was fundamentally a medieval miniatures wargame.

Its main objective was to provide procedures for representing medieval military battles on a table.

It included different scales and procedures related to:

- troops;
- movement;
- combat;
- armor;
- weapons;
- morale;
- formations;
- terrain;
- sieges;
- individual combat;
- tournaments.

Representation continued to belong to the wargaming paradigm:

```text
MINIATURES
     +
TERRAIN
     +
DISTANCES
     +
TROOP TYPES
     +
RULES
     ↓
SIMULATED BATTLE
```

The player continued to control primarily military forces.

We were not yet at:

```text
CHARACTER
    ↓
PERSISTENT WORLD
    ↓
EXPLORATION
    ↓
ADVENTURE
```

That jump belongs to another part of our genealogy.

## 6. Different scales of combat

One of the interesting aspects of *Chainmail* is that it was not limited to a single resolution scale.

There were rules for unit combats, but also procedures to represent *man-to-man* confrontations, where each miniature could represent an individual combatant.

The individual combat system used 2d6 and a matrix relating weapon types to armor levels to determine the required attack result.

This is historically interesting because it reduces the scale:

```text
MILITARY UNIT
       ↓
INDIVIDUAL COMBATANT
```

But we must avoid drawing conclusions too quickly.

**NOT DEMONSTRATED**

> Individual-scale combat does not automatically turn the combatant into an RPG character.

There can exist:

tactical individualization

without existing yet:

persistent character identity.

This will be a fundamental difference when we compare *Chainmail* with *Blackmoor*.

## 7. The Fantasy Supplement

Here appears the stratum of *Chainmail* that will have greater importance for our genealogy.

The *Fantasy Supplement* introduced procedures to incorporate fantastic elements into battles.

Among them appeared:

- Heroes;
- Super-Heroes;
- Wizards;
- Elves;
- Orcs;
- Dragons;
- Elementals;
- Trolls;
- fantastic creatures;
- magic weapons;
- spells.

We also find names that would immediately be familiar to later *D&D* players:

- Fireball;
- Lightning Bolt;
- light and darkness effects;
- summoning;
- resistance to certain magical effects.

The supplement also introduced an opposition between Law and Chaos, related to contemporary fantasy literary traditions.

Suddenly, the medieval battlefield could contain:

```text
KNIGHTS
    +
HEROES
    +
WIZARDS
    +
DRAGONS
    +
SPELLS
```

Still it was a wargame.

But its vocabulary was beginning to resemble what the fantasy RPG would later use.

## 8. Heroes and Super-Heroes

*Heroes* constitute a particularly interesting case.

In the *Fantasy Supplement*, a Hero could be roughly equivalent to about four ordinary combatants in certain combat situations, while the *Super-Hero* represented an even more powerful figure.

Conceptually something important appears:

> An exceptional individual can break the equivalence between a miniature and an ordinary soldier.

We now have individual figures with capabilities far superior to common units.

However:

**CAUTION**

> We should not automatically project over these *Heroes* the later concept of a *D&D* character.

They still belong to the context of a miniature battle.

What is interesting archaeologically is to observe how wargaming began to dispose of vocabulary to represent extraordinary individuals.

*Blackmoor* will take individualization much further.

## 9. The Wizard: magic converted into procedure

The *Wizard* is perhaps even more significant.

Introducing a wizard into a wargame requires resolving a new problem.

A conventional unit can be described through:

- movement;
- protection;
- attack;
- morale.

But a wizard can declare:

> "I cast a fireball."

This requires turning a fantastic action into a reproducible procedure.

The ruleset needs to determine:

```text
what can do
      ↓
range
      ↓
target
      ↓
effect
      ↓
resistance
      ↓
result
```

Here we find a characteristic that would later be fundamental for RPGs and, still later, for CRPGs:

> converting fantasy fiction into executable rules.

Magic stops being exclusively literary description.

It becomes a system.

## 10. Chainmail as formalization

Here probably appears the main conceptual contribution of *Chainmail* for our research.

Wargaming needed to transform imagined situations into shared procedures.

*Chainmail* fully belongs to that tradition:

```text
FICTIONAL SITUATION
       ↓
CATEGORIZATION
       ↓
RULE
       ↓
TABLE / DICE
       ↓
RESULT
```

This is different from the model we observed in *Blackmoor*, where many situations could be resolved through direct referee adjudication.

We can begin to see two trends:

```text
BLACKMOOR


situation
   ↓
referee
   ↓
interpretation
   ↓
result

versus:

CHAINMAIL


situation
   ↓
written procedure
   ↓
dice / tables
   ↓
result
```

This opposition is useful.

But it should not become a caricature.

Arneson also designed rules.

Gygax also designed worlds, situations, and game ideas.

The difference describes trends and backgrounds, not two incompatible personalities.

## 11. Arneson and Gygax were already connected

The two branches didn't have to wait for *D&D* to come into contact.

Arneson belonged to the *Castle & Crusade Society*, organization founded by Gary Gygax and Robert Kuntz.

Enthusiasts exchanged:

- letters;
- fanzines;
- rules;
- maps;
- campaigns;
- ideas.

Arneson and Gygax maintained correspondence before *D&D* existed.

The wargaming community provided the infrastructure that allowed the encounter.

## 12. Blackmoor arrives at Lake Geneva

One of the fundamental events of our genealogy occurred when Arneson showed *Blackmoor* to Gygax.

The exact chronology of some early encounters and demonstrations has been reconstructed in different ways by later sources, so it is wise to be cautious about overly precise dates.

What is clear enough is:

> Gygax directly got to know the type of game Arneson was developing in *Blackmoor*.

That was different from a conventional miniature battle.

There were:

- characters;
- exploration;
- dungeon;
- progression;
- adventures;
- campaign;
- referee.

Gygax recognized that something with enormous potential existed there.

## 13. The convergence

From then on, a collaboration process began.

Arneson and Gygax exchanged:

- rules;
- notes;
- ideas;
- manuscripts;
- comments;
- playtest results.

Development was carried out through correspondence and distant conversations, accompanied by playtesting in different groups.

Between 1973 and 1974, both collaborated on the work that would end up becoming *Dungeons & Dragons*.

Here our genealogy stops having two independent branches.

```text
BLACKMOOR                CHAINMAIL
    │                        │
    │                        │
    ▼                        ▼
 ARNESON                  GYGAX
    │                        │
    └───────────┬────────────┘
                │
                ▼
         COLLABORATION
                │
       rules + playtests
       writing + revision
                │
                ▼
      DUNGEONS & DRAGONS
```

## 14. It was not simply Blackmoor + Chainmail

The formula:

> *Blackmoor* + *Chainmail* = *D&D*

is attractive.

But it's too simple.

*D&D* incorporated ideas from numerous places:

- wargaming;
- *Braunstein*;
- *Blackmoor*;
- *Chainmail*;
- fantasy literature;
- previous campaigns;
- experiments by other players;
- new rules developed specifically during the process.

Moreover, some procedures used in *Blackmoor* were replaced.

Other *Chainmail* concepts disappeared or were deeply transformed.

Therefore:

```text
BLACKMOOR + CHAINMAIL
         ≠
    FULL D&D
```

Both are fundamental layers, not exclusive ingredients.

## 15. The problem they faced

*Blackmoor* had demonstrated that the experience worked.

But there was an enormous difference between:

playing *Blackmoor* with Dave Arneson

and:

buying a rulebook and trying to create your own *Blackmoor*.

The new product had to transfer knowledge.

That required answering questions like:

- what is a character?
- what attributes does it have?
- how does it improve?
- how does it fight?
- how does armor work?
- what can a wizard do?
- how is a monster represented?
- how is a dungeon explored?
- what does the referee do?
- how is a campaign created?
- how is uncertainty resolved?

The challenge was not only designing a game.

It was describing a new way of playing.

## 16. Dungeons & Dragons appears

In January 1974 was published:

*Dungeons & Dragons: Rules for Fantastic Medieval Wargames Campaigns Playable with Paper and Pencil and Miniature Figures*

The subtitle is archaeologically revealing.

*D&D* was still described using the vocabulary of the world it came from:

> Fantastic Medieval Wargames Campaigns

The modern term *role-playing game* was not yet the established commercial category it would later become.

The new genre was appearing before there was fully consolidated vocabulary to describe it.

## 17. Three small volumes

The first edition appeared in a box containing three small books:

- *Men & Magic*
- *Monsters & Treasure*
- *The Underworld & Wilderness Adventures*

The division is fascinating because it almost allows observing the architecture of the new game.

```text
MEN & MAGIC
characters + abilities + magic


MONSTERS & TREASURE
content + risk + reward


UNDERWORLD & WILDERNESS
space + exploration + campaign
```

Character.

World.

Procedures.

The RPG was beginning to become a document.

## 18. What D&D formalized

*OD&D* provided structures for elements like:

- characters;
- classes;
- levels;
- experience;
- attributes;
- combat;
- armor;
- magic;
- spells;
- monsters;
- treasures;
- movement;
- exploration;
- dungeon;
- wilderness;
- campaigns.

An experience that previously could depend enormously on the referee's implicit knowledge began to have a shared vocabulary.

Now groups separated by geography could talk about:

- Fighter.
- Magic-User.
- Level.
- Armor Class.
- Hit Points.
- Experience Points.

The new game was beginning to be portable.

## 19. But D&D still needed the referee

Here we find a fundamental paradox.

*D&D* formalized the experience enormously.

But it did not attempt to eliminate the referee.

On the contrary.

Much of the game continued to depend on the referee's ability to:

- interpret situations;
- build the world;
- prepare maps;
- control monsters;
- manage information;
- decide consequences;
- resolve unanticipated actions.

The model continued being:

```text
PLAYER
   │
   │ declares intention
   ▼
REFEREE
   │
   ├── consult rules
   ├── interpret
   ├── adjudicate
   └── update world
   │
   ▼
RESULT
```

Two centuries after *Kriegsspiel*, the umpire figure was still alive.

It had just changed scenery.

## 20. The Dungeon Master as a human machine

From the future perspective of the CRPG, we can describe the referee in a deliberately mechanical way.

It receives:

**INPUT**

"Abro la puerta."
"Ataco al orco."
"Busco una trampa."
"Lanzo *Fireball*."

It queries:

**STATE**

- map
- characters
- enemies
- inventory
- rules
- hidden information

It executes:

**PROCEDURES**

- movement
- rolls
- combat
- detection
- magic
- reaction

It produces:

**OUTPUT**

"Encuentras..."
"Fallaste..."
"El orco..."
"La puerta..."

That is to say:

```text
INPUT
  ↓
STATE + RULES
  ↓
REFEREE
  ↓
RESULT
  ↓
NEW STATE
```

This observation will be fundamental later.

Because the computer will try to occupy precisely that space.

## 21. TSR

Publishing a new game required money, printing, and distribution.

Gary Gygax and Don Kaye founded *Tactical Studies Rules* (TSR) to publish *D&D*.

The first run was small.

But the game began to spread rapidly within the hobby.

Here another important transformation occurs:

```text
LOCAL EXPERIMENT
       ↓
RULESET
       ↓
PRODUCT
       ↓
DISTRIBUTION
       ↓
NEW GROUPS
       ↓
NEW CAMPAIGNS
```

Knowledge no longer needed to be transmitted only through personal contact.

It could travel inside a box.

## 22. The game escapes its creators

This might be one of the most important moments of our genealogy.

*Blackmoor* depended enormously on Arneson.

Gygax's campaigns depended on Gygax.

But *D&D* could produce:

```text
PERSON WHO
NEVER MET
ARNESON OR GYGAX
       ↓
BUYS D&D
       ↓
INTERPRETS RULES
       ↓
CREATES DUNGEON
       ↓
RUNS CAMPAIGN
       ↓
OTHER PLAYERS
CREATE CHARACTERS
```

The new way of playing had become reproducible at a distance.

That is a gigantic historical difference.

## 23. An incomplete rulebook

However, the 1974 *OD&D* was not a particularly pedagogical modern manual.

Its text assumed some wargaming knowledge and left many procedures open to interpretation.

It even referred to *Chainmail* as an option for certain combat procedures, although it also included what was called the *Alternative Combat System*, based on the d20.

This reveals something important.

*D&D* was still between two worlds:

```text
WARGAME
   │
   │
   ▼
OD&D 1974
   │
   │
   ▼
RPG AS
AUTONOMOUS GENRE
```

The product existed.

But its language was still learning how to explain what it was.

## 24. Two styles still present in the game

The collaboration between Arneson and Gygax did not eliminate the two trends we have observed.

The RPG continued to contain simultaneously:

**Adjudication**

> The player tries something unexpected and the referee decides how to resolve it.

**System**

> The player performs an anticipated action and the rulebook provides a procedure.

Therefore:

```text
              RPG
               │
       ┌───────┴───────┐
       │               │
       ▼               ▼
   ADJUDICATION      RULES
       │               │
       ▼               ▼
   FLEXIBILITY     CONSISTENCY
```

The tension between both extremes will not disappear.

In fact, it will continue to accompany RPG design for decades.

## 25. Arneson enters TSR

After the initial success of *D&D*, Dave Arneson ended up working for TSR in 1976.

His tenure at the company was brief.

Later sources describe:

- methodological differences;
- organizational problems;
- personal disagreements;
- different expectations;
- conflicts over production and work.

Some testimonies have emphasized Arneson's difficulties adapting to editorial deadlines.

Other participants from that time offer a more complex image of TSR's internal culture.

**CAUTION**

> We should not reduce his departure to: "Arneson didn't meet *deadlines* and TSR fired him."

The available evidence points to a much more complex relationship.

The matter deserves to be studied through specific documentation if we want to reconstruct it with greater precision in the future.

## 26. The transformation of TSR

TSR was leaving behind a small operation created to publish a strange game.

*D&D* was growing.

Appeared:

- supplements;
- new rules;
- products;
- employees;
- production needs;
- contracts;
- intellectual property;
- editorial calendars.

The ecosystem had changed.

In Twin Cities, a rule could be modified during the next session.

In a publishing house:

```text
IDEA
  ↓
MANUSCRIPT
  ↓
REVIEW
  ↓
LAYOUT
  ↓
PRINTING
  ↓
DISTRIBUTION
```

needed to become a finished product.

The experimental culture of the hobby was coming into contact with the demands of an industry.

## 27. Gygax and written production

In this transition, one characteristic of Gary Gygax was especially important:

> his enormous written production.

Before and after *D&D*, he produced:

- articles;
- rules;
- supplements;
- scenarios;
- correspondence;
- editorial material.

This capability was especially suitable for a company whose raw material consisted precisely of turning game ideas into publishable documents.

This does not make Gygax simply:

> "the one who wrote Arneson's ideas."

His participation in design was substantial.

But it helps explain why his role grew as *D&D* became an editorial line.

## 28. Advanced Dungeons & Dragons

During the second half of the 1970s, a new branch appeared:

*Advanced Dungeons & Dragons*.

The main books were published under Gary Gygax's authorship.

*AD&D* represented, among other things, a much greater systematization of the game.

But it also opened a fundamental contractual problem.

Was *AD&D*:

a new and independent game?

Or was it:

an evolution or derivation of *Dungeons & Dragons*?

The answer had economic consequences.

Dave Arneson had contractual rights related to *D&D*.

If *AD&D* belonged to that same contractual family, there could be royalties to claim.

## 29. The conflict

At the end of the 1970s, Arneson initiated legal actions related to *D&D* and *AD&D*.

The conflict mainly affected:

- royalties;
- contracts;
- authorship;
- derivative nature of certain products;
- recognition.

Therefore, it's incorrect to summarize it only as:

> "Arneson sued to get his name on it."

The conflict was also economic and contractual.

And it was closely related to a fundamental question:

> Whose was the work that had been born from that collaboration?

## 30. Do not turn the dispute into mythology

Decades of discussion have produced two recurring caricatures.

**Mythology A**

> Arneson invented everything and Gygax took it.

**Mythology B**

> Arneson had some ideas, but Gygax really created *D&D* because he was able to write it down.

Both explanations are too simple.

The documentation we have studied shows a much more interesting process.

Arneson contributed a decisive experience developed around *Blackmoor*.

Gygax contributed considerable experience as a designer, writer, and hobby organizer.

Both worked together on *D&D*.

And both were using ideas developed within much older communities.

History doesn't need a single hero.

## 31. Authorship as an archaeological problem

The question:

> "Who invented *D&D*?"

might be poorly formulated.

We can replace it with several better questions:

- Who developed certain practices?
- Where are they first documented?
- Who turned them into rules?
- Who integrated them?
- Who published them?
- Who made others able to reproduce them?

Then a much richer chain appears:

```text
COMMUNITY
    ↓
EXPERIMENT
    ↓
PRACTICE
    ↓
RULE
    ↓
DOCUMENTATION
    ↓
PRODUCT
    ↓
PUBLISHING
```

*D&D* is the result of that entire process.

## 32. The work of Arneson and Gygax

So, what did they do together?

Our provisional conclusion would be:

> Arneson and Gygax managed to turn a series of experimental practices from wargaming, campaigns, adjudication, and fantasy into a product that allowed other groups to create their own worlds, characters, and adventures.

Arneson does not contribute simply "imagination."

Gygax does not contribute simply "the rules."

Both design.

Both adapt.

Both come from previous communities.

But their convergent trajectories help explain why *D&D* achieved what *Blackmoor* and *Chainmail* separately had not:

> making a new way of playing reproducible.

## 33. What Dungeons & Dragons left behind

From the perspective of RPG Archaeology, the importance of *D&D* does not lie only in having created an industry.

*D&D* consolidated a set of extraordinarily fertile abstractions:

```text
CHARACTER
CLASS
LEVEL
EXPERIENCE
HIT POINTS
ARMOR CLASS
SPELL
MONSTER
TREASURE
DUNGEON
PARTY
CAMPAIGN
```

Not all were invented from scratch by *D&D*.

But *D&D* gathered them all into a shared and enormously influential system.

And that vocabulary would be especially important when the computer appears.

## 34. The next problem: eliminating the referee

Here begins our real bridge toward the CRPG.

In a *D&D* table:

```text
               PLAYERS
                    │
                    ▼
               CHARACTERS
                    │
                    ▼
                 REFEREE
            ┌───────┼───────┐
            ▼       ▼       ▼
           MAP     RULES   WORLD
            │       │       │
            └───────┼───────┘
                    ▼
                RESULT
```

But a computer does not initially have a human capable of interpreting any intention.

Someone will have to transform:

> "What do you want to do?"

into:

1. MOVE
2. ATTACK
3. CAST
4. USE
5. OPEN
6. TAKE

The open freedom of tabletop will have to become a finite set of commands.

## 35. From the referee to the algorithm

Here our entire genealogy reappears in a new form.

```text
KRIEGSSPIEL
     │
     ▼
UMPIRE
     │
     ▼
FREE KRIEGSSPIEL
     │
     ▼
BRAUNSTEIN
     │
     ▼
REFEREE
     │
     ▼
BLACKMOOR
     │
     ▼
DUNGEON MASTER
     │
     ▼
DUNGEONS & DRAGONS
     │
     ▼

```

The next layer will have to replace:

**REFEREE**

with:

**SOFTWARE**

And then a fascinating problem appears.

The referee can understand:

> "I try to distract the guard by rolling a coin down the hallway."

The 1970s computer couldn't.

Therefore, the first CRPG will have to decide:

> Which parts of *D&D* are important enough to be coded?

We can interpret *D&D* in a deliberately anachronistic but useful way.

The ruleset provides:

```text

CON

 DAMAGE
```

To a programmer, this begins to look suspiciously familiar.

*D&D* had already performed part of an essential operation:

> converting fantasy into manipulable abstractions.

## 37. What the computer will inherit easily

Some parts of *D&D* are extraordinarily easy to imagine as software:

- Hit Points
- Experience Points
- Levels
- Armor Class
- Attack tables
- Saving throws
- Spell slots
- Inventory
- Monster statistics
- Dungeon coordinates
- Random encounters
- Treasure tables

They are numbers, states, tables, and procedures.

The computer is extraordinarily good with them.

## 38. What the computer will lose

Other parts are much more difficult.

Especially:

open adjudication.

The human referee can respond to an action that no one had anticipated.

The computer can only execute what the programmer has represented.

Therefore:

```text
TABLETOP RPG


narrative possibilities
        ↓
      enormous
        ↓
     referee

will initially become:

COMPUTER RPG


programmed actions
        ↓
   limited set
        ↓
     algorithm
```

The CRPG gains:

- speed;
- calculation;
- automatic persistence;
- representation;
- constant simulation.

But it loses a huge part of interpretive freedom.

## 39. The dungeon as a perfect candidate

Here we understand even better why the dungeon crawler is so important to CRPG history.

The dungeon already has an easily formalizable structure:

```text
ROOM
  │
CORRIDOR
  │
DOOR
  │
ROOM
```

It can easily become:

```text
GRID
 │
CELL
 │
EDGE
 │
ENTITY
```

And the most frequent actions can also be reduced:

- MOVE
- TURN
- OPEN
- ATTACK
- CAST
- TAKE

The dungeon limits the possibility space enough for a primitive computer to represent it.

It's not a coincidence that so many early attempts to transfer *D&D* to computer focus precisely on dungeons.

## 41. Archaeological synthesis

After tracing from *Kriegsspiel* to *D&D*, we can formulate a provisional conclusion.

*Dungeons & Dragons* did not appear as an isolated invention.

It was the result of decades of evolution and the convergence of different communities.

In the Twin Cities, Wesley and other wargamers experimented with individual characters, asymmetric information, and referee adjudication.

Arneson turned part of that tradition into *Blackmoor*, where characters and world gained persistence.

In Lake Geneva, Perren and Gygax worked on formalizing medieval and fantasy combat through *Chainmail*.

Arneson and Gygax connected both trajectories and collaborated to produce *D&D*.

The great historical innovation of the product can be stated as:

> An experience that depended on specific communities and specific referees became a set of documented abstractions that strangers could use to build their own campaigns.

*D&D* did not eliminate the referee.

But it formalized enough of the world surrounding the referee to make the game transmittable.

## 44. Sources and documentary criteria

For this document, we maintain the same source hierarchy used in previous investigations.

### Contemporary documentation

Should receive priority when available:

- original editions of *Chainmail*;
- 1974 *OD&D*;
- fanzines;
- correspondence;
- *Castle & Crusade Society* documents;
- contracts;
- legal documentation;
- preserved maps and notes.

### Participant testimonies

They are especially valuable to reconstruct practices that were never formally written down.

The documentary:

[*Secrets of Blackmoor*](https://secretsofblackmoor.com/)

is particularly useful to reconstruct the Twin Cities ecosystem and contrast later narratives about Wesley, Arneson, and *Blackmoor*.

It should be classified as retrospective testimony, not contemporary documentation.

### Secondary historical research

Modern reconstructions are especially useful for relating scattered documents, but controversial claims about authorship, priority, or causality should always be contrasted when possible.
